How this works, and where it breaks

Turning five separate numbers into one ranking takes two decisions. Neither has a right answer. This page says what they are instead of hiding them in an appendix.

Decision 1: putting different numbers on the same scale

The measures do not share a scale. A grade-8 maths score of 283 and a guidance status of “published” are not the same kind of number, so before they can be averaged, something has to decide what counts as 0 and what counts as 100.

Best state = 100. The top state gets 100, the bottom gets 0, everyone else sits in between. Easy to read, and what most published rankings use. The catch: if every state scores between 78 and 82, this stretches that small gap across the whole scale and makes near-ties look like blowouts.

Keep the real spacing. States are placed by how far they sit from the average, so a genuinely middling state lands in the middle instead of being pushed to an extreme. The catch: the scale has no natural top or bottom.

There is no principled reason for those numbers. We picked them. Every published ranking picks them too — most just don't say so.

How we test whether a rank means anything

Instead of trusting one set of weights, we build a thousand rankings with the weights chosen at random, and record where each state lands every time. A state that stays put across all of them has a rank worth reporting. A state that bounces around does not.

The weights are drawn completely at random on purpose. Picking a set of “sensible” alternatives would mean making the same kind of judgement this whole page is questioning.

Missing numbers

We never fill one in. If a state is missing a measure, it is scored on the ones it has, and the table shows what share of the evidence that score rests on. Guessing a missing value would mean inventing a measurement, which is the thing this project is complaining about.

What this does not show

It does not show that technology, AI policy or money causes better schools. A state near the top has good test scores, has published guidance, and spends a certain amount per pupil. That is all it means.

An earlier version of this page said the measures were tangled together because richer states do better on all of them at once. That turned out to be worth checking rather than asserting. Across the 50 states, per-pupil spending and NAEP achievement correlate at r = 0.22 — weak. New York spends the most in the country and sits mid-table on results; Idaho spends the least and scores above New York.

So the measures do not simply move together, and adding spending made the ranking less stable rather than more — the typical swing went from 14 places to 15, and the full range from 20 to 26. Note the limit of that result too: spending per pupil is not the same as a state being wealthy, since it also reflects what teaching and buildings cost locally.

Where it is weak

  • The test scores are from one year. NAEP runs every two years and 2024 is the latest, so this is a snapshot. It cannot tell you whether anything is improving.
  • “No guidance found” is not proof there is none. It means the tracker we used does not list any. That is weaker evidence than it looks.
  • Six measures is still not many. Home internet and device access would belong here and are not in yet.
  • Test scores measure schools, not AI readiness. They are the best comparable data available across all 50 states, which is not the same as being the right measure.
