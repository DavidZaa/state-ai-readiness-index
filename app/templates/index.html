State AI Readiness Index

Rankings look like facts. They are choices.

Reports rank states on how ready they are for AI in schools. Every one of them picks what to measure, then picks how much each thing counts. Someone chooses those numbers.

Change them and the order changes. Most reports never show you that. This site hands you the controls, and measures how much the ranking moves when you use them.

All the data is public, and every number links to where it came from.

What is actually being measured

What a ranking like this is

Take a few numbers about each state, put them on a common scale, decide how much each one counts, add them up, and sort. That is all any of these rankings are, including this one. The result looks like a measurement but it is arithmetic applied to a set of decisions.

What “AI guidance” means

Some state education departments have published official advice for schools on using AI — what teachers may use it for, how to handle cheating, what happens to student data. Thirty-five states have. Fifteen have not. It says a state has engaged with the question, not that any classroom changed.

Why these six numbers

Four are test scores: maths and reading, at grades 4 and 8, from NAEP — the one exam given identically in every state. One is whether the state published AI guidance. One is what the state spends per pupil. Between them they cover results, policy, and money.

Why compare states at all

Because these comparisons get used. They are quoted in news coverage, cited when budgets are argued over, and pointed at when a state wants to claim it is ahead. If a state's position shifts by fourteen places depending on choices nobody published, those arguments are standing on very little.
