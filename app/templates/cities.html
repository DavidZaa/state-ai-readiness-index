The same question, for cities

NAEP gives the same test to a group of large urban school districts that it gives to the states. That makes a city ranking possible — and it inherits the same problem, plus two of its own.

Read this before the table

  • These are school districts, not cities. Several cover a whole county and are named for it. A district boundary is not a city boundary, and the population inside it is not the city's population.
  • The AI guidance measure is inherited from the state. Guidance is published by state education departments, so a district has no status of its own here. That its state published something is a real fact about its policy environment; it is not evidence the district adopted anything. New York City is the exception, and it runs the other way — New York State is coded partial precisely because the guidance on the tracker is New York City's own, so for this district it counts in full.
  • Five measures here, six on the states page. Per-pupil spending is published by state, and the districts NAEP assesses do not line up cleanly with the finance data, so it is left out rather than approximated.
  • Twenty-five districts, not fifty states. A shorter list makes each rank move less, so the swing numbers here are not comparable with the states page.
